Household Sound Detection

Technical evidence summary, October 2026: methods, results and limitations.
Summary. On a 15-sound household benchmark with simulated noise, room echo and very short clips, our fine-tuned model detects 56% of real events at a 1% false-alarm rate, against 43% for Apple's built-in sound classifier. It retains 54% after compression to 8-bit precision, small enough for low-cost embedded chips. On clean studio-quality audio, Apple's classifier is slightly ahead. All results to date use public recordings; real-home validation is the next step.

1. What we have built

  • Smoke and CO alarm detector. Identifies the standard alarm beep rhythm (ISO 8201: three beeps for smoke, four for carbon monoxide) rather than relying on a sound label. Implemented in Swift for iPhone and in C for embedded chips, with identical results on shared test files. It detected 3 of 12 real fire-alarm recordings; the other 9 are bells, horns or 2-second snippets with no beep pattern, which remain a gap.
  • On-phone model. A 17 KB model for glass breaking, knock, doorbell and baby crying, running alongside Apple's classifier inside the app with a per-sound rule for how many consecutive sound windows must agree before an alert.
  • 15-sound networks. Public EfficientAT networks (MIT licence) fine-tuned end to end on commercially licensed audio. A small network (0.72 million weights, 0.72 MB at 8-bit) and a large one (4.2 million weights, 4.2 MB at 8-bit). Sounds: alarm, baby crying, cough, dog bark, door slam, doorbell, glass breaking, gunshot, knock, microwave, phone ring, scream, siren, toilet flush and running water.
  • Opt-in clip review. A "Sounds heard" page keeps a 5-second clip of each alert on the user's phone only, so the user can mark it Right or Wrong. This is the mechanism for collecting real-home evaluation data.

2. Main result: hard-conditions benchmark

Each sound is scored in six conditions built from held-out FSD50K evaluation clips: clean, noise at 10 dB, noise at 0 dB, room echo, 1-second blips, and noise, echo and short clips combined. Noise and echo are simulated to approximate home conditions. The metric is the share of real events detected when only 1% of non-events may trigger a false alarm, so no model benefits from a favourably chosen alert threshold.

4. Independent check: ESC-50, clean recordings

ESC-50 is a standard 2,000-clip benchmark that we did not train on. Seven sounds overlap with ours (dog, cough, toilet, siren, glass, knock, baby crying); the negatives are the other 1,720 clips.

ModelDetected at 1% false alarmsAUC
Apple built-in classifier98.6%0.998
Ours, large, full precision96.1%0.998
Public EfficientAT mn20 as released (no training by us)93.2%0.996
Ours, small, 8-bit90.7%0.994

On clean, standard data every model is near the ceiling and Apple's classifier is marginally best. Our advantage is in degraded conditions: noise, echo, faint and very short sounds.

7. Comparison with a general-purpose audio language model

We also tested OpenAI's audio model (gpt-audio-1.5), which takes real audio, exactly as released and with no examples. Both systems were given the same 500 clips (10 clean and 10 hard recordings of each of the 15 sounds, plus 100 clean and 100 hard background clips) and had to pick one of 16 labels. Chance is not 1 in 16 here: answering "background" every time scores 40%.

SystemAll 500 clipsSounds, cleanSounds, hard conditionsBackground
Ours (large, 8-bit)59%81%54%46%
OpenAI gpt-audio-1.548%67%29%48%

The audio model is respectable on clean recordings (very good on baby crying and running water) but falls away in hard conditions, and it found 2 of 20 alarms and 5 of 20 gunshots. Nineteen of its answers were not a usable label and are counted as wrong.

Read with care: this is a different, simpler measure from sections 2 to 5 (one label per clip, not a false-alarm-controlled alert), our model's answer is its highest-scoring label averaged over the clip, and our model was trained on this kind of audio while the language model was not. The result shows what a general-purpose model achieves with no tuning, not a ceiling for such models. A cloud audio model would also need continuous home audio sent off the device.

8. Limitations

  • Real-home validation is pending. Noise and echo are simulated. Training and test clips both come from FSD50K, as separate sets, so the hard-conditions benchmark is more favourable to us than a fully independent one; ESC-50 (section 4) is the independent check.
  • Comparison scope. We measured Apple's classifier, one untrained public network and one general-purpose audio language model. Stronger public models (BEATs, PaSST) and other audio language models are untested, and we do not claim state of the art.
  • Alert settings are conservative. At up to one false alarm per hour, calibrated settings detect roughly 30 to 50% of events in a crowded stream for the stronger sounds (glass 12 of 32, dog 21 of 32), and few of door slam, doorbell, generic alarm and scream.
  • Embedded hardware. Model sizes and operation counts are calculated; they have not yet been measured on a target chip.
  • Data licensing. Training uses only CC0 and CC BY clips and Donate-a-Cry (ODbL). ESC-50 (CC BY-NC) is used for testing only. Attribution will be included at release.

9. Next steps

  • Collect real-home clips through the opt-in Sounds heard feature and rerun this benchmark on them.
  • Learn each home's own sounds (doorbell, washing machine, kettle) from signals the user already gives, such as opening the door after a doorbell alert.
  • Evaluate larger public networks and other audio language models (with example clips), run the compressed model on the target chip, and compare directly against competing devices.
